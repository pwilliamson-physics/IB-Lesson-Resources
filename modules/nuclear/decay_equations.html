Decay Equations

By the end of this workbook you should be able to:
  • Write the general equations for alpha and beta-minus decay
  • Use mass numbers and atomic numbers to complete a decay equation
  • Identify the daughter nucleus from its atomic number
  • Include the electron antineutrino in a beta-minus decay equation

1. Alpha decay

Radioactive nuclei are not stable. They become more stable by emitting (giving out) energy in the form of radiation and particles.

In alpha decay the nucleus emits an alpha particle: 2 protons and 2 neutrons, which is the same as a helium nucleus.

AZX→A−4Z−2Y+42α

The alpha particle is shown as the symbol α above, but as a helium nucleus below. In both cases the equation is identical.

AZX→A−4Z−2Y+42He
Balancing rule: the top numbers (mass numbers) must add up to the same total on both sides of the arrow, and so must the bottom numbers (atomic numbers). Use a periodic table to find the element symbol that matches an atomic number.
Worked example 1.1

Radium-226 decays by alpha emission. Mass number: 226 − 4 = 222. Atomic number: 88 − 2 = 86, which is radon (Rn).

22688Ra→22286Rn+42α

2. Beta decay

In beta-minus decay a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton. The nucleus emits an electron (the beta particle) and an electron antineutrino.

AZX→AZ+1Y+0−1β+00νe

The beta particle is shown as the symbol β above, but as an electron below. In both cases the equation is identical.

AZX→AZ+1Y+0−1e+00νe
The electron antineutrino, νe. It has no charge and almost no mass, so its mass number and charge number are both 0. It doesn't change the totals on either side, but a complete beta-minus decay equation must include it.
Worked example 2.1

Iodine-131 decays by beta-minus emission. The mass number stays at 131. Atomic number: 53 + 1 = 54, which is xenon (Xe).

13153I→13154Xe+0−1β+00νe
