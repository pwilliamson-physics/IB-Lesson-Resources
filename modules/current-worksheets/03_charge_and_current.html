Charge, Current and Time

By the end of this workbook you should be able to:
  • use Q = I t and I = Q / t, converting between minutes/hours and seconds, and between mA/µA and A
  • count the number of electrons making up a given charge using n = Q / e
  • write and interpret answers in scientific notation, including very large and very small values
  • describe, in your own words, what a semiconductor and a superconductor are, and how lightning happens

1. Getting started

Formulae you'll need: Q = I t  |  I = Q / t  |  number of charge carriers n = Q / e, where e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Type numeric answers as plain numbers, to 2–3 significant figures. For scientific-notation answers, type them as e.g. 7.25 x 10^-5 or 7.25 × 10^(-5) — spaces are ignored and ×/x/* all work, but a bare 7.25E-5 or 7.25x10-5 (no ^ or brackets) won't be accepted.
Every question has a hint if you get stuck, but the full working isn't shown — talk your method through with a classmate or your teacher if you're still unsure after using the hint.

These all use Q = I t or I = Q / t directly, with times already in seconds.

1The current in a wire is 0.35 A. Calculate the charge that passes a point in the wire in 10 s.
C
Use Q = I × t. The time is already in seconds, so you can substitute straight in.
2A charge of 15 C passes through a wire in 5 s. Calculate the average current.
A
Rearrange Q = I t to make I the subject.
3What is the average current in a wire when a charge of 150 C passes in 30 s?
A
I = Q / t.
4How long does it take for a charge of 6.0 C to pass a point in a circuit when the current is 0.5 A?
s
Rearrange Q = I t to make t the subject.

2. Watch your units

Same formulae, but now you'll need to convert minutes to seconds, or mA/µA to A, before you substitute.

5The current in the same wire (0.35 A) — calculate the charge passing a point in 10 minutes.
C
Convert minutes to seconds first: 1 minute = 60 s.
6A charge of 15 C passes in 100 s instead. Calculate the average current.
A
I = Q / t — no conversion needed here, just watch your decimal point.
7A charge of 3500 C passes a point in 9 s. Calculate the average current.
A
I = Q / t. This one gives quite a large current — that's expected.
8The same 3500 C passes a point in 200 s instead. Calculate the average current.
A
Same formula as question 7, different time.
9The current in a wire is 0.72 A. Calculate the charge that passes through the wire in 4 s.
C
Q = I × t.
10Using the same current (0.72 A), calculate the charge that passes through the wire in 7 minutes.
C
7 minutes = 420 seconds.
11In an electron beam experiment, the beam current is 1.2 mA. Calculate the charge flowing along the beam each minute.
C
1.2 mA = 1.2 × 10⁻³ A. 1 minute = 60 s.
12The current in a small torch bulb is 0.20 A. Calculate the total charge that passes a point in the circuit in 12 minutes.
C
Convert the 12 minutes to seconds before using Q = I t.
13In an old-fashioned television tube (a CRT), the picture is formed by a stream of electrons hitting the screen, with a current of 20 mA. How much charge hits the screen in 30 minutes?
C
20 mA = 0.020 A. 30 minutes = 1800 s.
14How long does it take for a charge of 48 C to pass a point in a circuit if the current is 36 mA?
s
Convert 36 mA to amps before you divide.

3. Bigger problems

Multi-part questions, and some less familiar numbers.

15A certain type of rechargeable battery can deliver a current of 0.2 A for 4000 s before its voltage drops and it needs recharging.
a) Total charge the battery can deliver before it needs recharging: C
b) Time it could be used for without recharging if the current through it were 0.5 A: s
c) Time it could be used for at a current of 0.1 A: s
First find the total charge in part (a). Then use that fixed value of Q in Q = I t for parts (b) and (c), rearranged to find t.
16In a lightning flash, a typical amount of charge reaching the Earth is 10 C. If the flash lasts for 0.50 ms, what is the average current?
A
0.50 ms = 0.50 × 10⁻³ s. Expect a very large answer — lightning currents really are huge.

4. Counting electrons

A coulomb is a huge number of individual electrons. To count them, find the charge Q first, then use n = Q / e, where e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Typing scientific notation: write it as e.g. 3.75 x 10^15, 3.75 × 10^15, or 3.75 x 10(15) — spaces don't matter and ×/x/* are all accepted, but there must be a clear ^ or brackets around the exponent. A calculator-style 3.75E15 or a concatenated 3.75x1015 won't be marked correct.
17How many electrons pass a point in a wire in 10 minutes when the current is 1.0 µA?
electrons
Find Q = I t first (watch the µA and the minutes), then divide by e.
18How many electrons pass a point in a wire in 10 minutes when the current is 5.0 A?
electrons
Same method as question 17, much bigger current.
19For the 1.2 mA electron beam (question 11), how many electrons pass along the beam each minute?
electrons
You already found the charge for this one in question 11 — now divide by e.
20How many electrons would be needed to provide the 10 C of charge in the lightning flash (question 16)?
electrons
n = Q / e, using the 10 C given in the question.
21How many electrons passed through the wire carrying 0.72 A in 4 s (question 9)?
electrons
Use your answer to question 9 as the charge Q.
22How many electrons passed through the wire carrying 0.72 A in 7 minutes (question 10)?
electrons
Use your answer to question 10 as the charge Q.
23How many electrons make up a charge of 3500 C?
electrons
n = Q / e directly — you're given Q already.
24How many electrons pass this point in the torch bulb circuit in 12 minutes (question 12)?
electrons
Use your answer to question 12 as the charge Q.
25Calculate the number of electrons passing a point in a wire in 1 minute when the current is 1 A.
electrons
Find Q for 1 A over 60 s first.
26Calculate the number of electrons passing a point in a wire in 1 minute when the current is 5.0 µA.
electrons
5.0 µA = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ A. Find Q first, then n = Q / e.
27Calculate the number of electrons passing a point in a circuit with a current of 4.0 A for 60 s.
electrons
Q = I t first, then n = Q / e.
28Calculate the number of electrons passing a point in a circuit with a current of 12 mA for 25 s.
electrons
12 mA = 12 × 10⁻³ A.

5. Extension: beyond the worksheets

29What is a semiconductor? What is a superconductor? Find a reliable source (your Hodder textbook or a trustworthy science website) and note down where your definition came from.
A semiconductor's resistivity sits between a conductor and an insulator, and changes a lot with temperature. A superconductor loses all electrical resistance below a very low "critical" temperature. Make sure you mention both ideas and give your source.
