Fields Test Review: Units D1–D4

By the end of this review you should be able to:
  • build the SI units of gravitational potential from force and work done (Test Q3)
  • use the right-hand grip rule to find the field around a wire, and add field vectors from two wires (Test Q5)

1. Units of gravitational potential Test Q3

Before tackling the exam question, build up the units step by step from equations you already know. Every SI unit can be written using just the base units kg, m and s (for mechanics).

How to type your answers: write the power of each base unit in its small box. Use a minus sign for negative powers (e.g. -2). If a base unit doesn't appear, type 0 or leave the box blank.
1What are the SI base units of force?
kgms
Start from F = ma. What are the units of mass, and of acceleration?
F = ma → units of kg × m s−2 = kg m s−2 (this is what 1 N means).
2What are the SI base units of work done (energy)?
kgms
Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. Multiply your answer to the previous question by the unit of distance.
W = Fs → kg m s−2 × m = kg m2 s−2 (this is what 1 J means).
Gravitational potential, Vg, at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point: Vg = W / m.
3Which is a correct unit for gravitational potential? Test Q3
Gravitational potential is work done per unit mass: divide your units for work done by kg, then compare with each option. Watch out for options that multiply by kg instead of dividing, or that are really the unit of field strength.
A. J kg−1 = m2 s−2.
B multiplies by kg instead of dividing. C (m s−2) is the unit of gravitational field strength (= N kg−1). D has m−1; N m kg−1 would have been correct.

2. The field between two wires Test Q5

P and Q are two long, parallel wires perpendicular to the page. They carry equal currents in opposite directions: the current in P is out of the page and the current in Q is into the page. R is a point the same distance from P and from Q.

Step 1 — the field around each wire

4Add direction arrows to the magnetic field lines around each wire.

Click (or tap) a wire’s field lines to add arrows. Click again to reverse them, and a third time to clear them.

Out of the page means your thumb points at your own face. Do your fingers curl clockwise or anticlockwise as you look at the page? Into the page is the opposite.
P (current out of page) → field lines go anticlockwise. Q (current into page) → field lines go clockwise.

Step 2 — the field at R from each wire separately

5What is the direction of the magnetic field at R due to wire P alone?
The field line around P that passes through R is a circle centred on P. At R, the field points along that circle (a tangent), so it is perpendicular to the line PR. Then use the arrow direction from Step 1. Out of the page means your thumb points at your own face. Using the right-hand grip rule, do your fingers curl clockwise or anticlockwise as you look at the page? Into the page is the opposite.
R is directly to the right of P. On an anticlockwise circle the right-hand side of the circle points up the page.
6What is the direction of the magnetic field at R due to wire Q alone?
R is directly above Q. Which way does a clockwise circle point at its top?
R is at the top of a clockwise circle centred on Q, so the field there points to the right.

Step 3 — the exam question

7What is the direction of the magnetic field produced at R? Test Q5
Add your two field vectors from Step 2. The currents are equal and R is the same distance from both wires, so the two fields have equal magnitude.
A. The field at R from P points up, and the field at R from Q points right, with equal magnitudes. Their resultant points up and to the right, at 45°, which is arrow A.

4. Force between two parallel wires Test Q12(a)

Two parallel wires, A and B, both carry a current into the page.

Stage 1 — the field around A

13Give the direction of the magnetic field lines around wire A.

Click (or tap) the field lines to add arrows. Click again to reverse them, and a third time to clear them.

Current into the page: point your right thumb into the screen. Which way do your fingers curl as you look at it?
Current into the page → field lines go clockwise. The mark scheme also expects the circles to be centred on A, with the gap between them getting bigger further out.
14In Fig. 4.1, how should the gap between neighbouring field lines change as you move outwards from A?
Closely spaced field lines mean a strong field. Where is the field of a wire strongest?
It should increase, because B ∝ 1/r and the field gets weaker further from the wire. Drawing at least three circles with gaps that grow outwards earned a mark in the test.

Stage 2 — now bring in wire B

15What is the direction of A’s magnetic field at the position of B?
B is directly to the right of A, on a clockwise circle. Which way does a clockwise circle point on its right-hand side?
On a clockwise circle, the right-hand side points down the page.
16What is the direction of the current in wire B?
Into the page (⊗), the same direction as the current in A.
17Using Fleming’s left-hand rule, what is the direction of the force on B?
First finger = field (down the page), second finger = current (into the page). Where does your thumb point?
To the left, towards A. The force acts in the plane of the paper.
18What is the direction of the force on A due to B?
Think about Newton’s third law, or repeat the steps above using B’s field at A.
To the right, towards B. The forces are equal and opposite (Newton’s third law), so wires with currents in the same direction attract.

Stage 3 — the exam question

19State and explain why a force acts on B due to A in the plane of the paper. [3] Test Q12(a)(ii)
  • B lies in the magnetic field of A ✓
  • Use of an appropriate rule (e.g. Fleming’s left-hand rule): A’s field at B points down the page and the current in B is into the page ✓
  • so the force on B is to the left (towards A), in the plane of the paper ✓
Also accepted: the field lines of A and B combine into one pattern with a null point between the wires. The wires move to reduce the stored energy, which they do by moving together, so the force on B is to the left.

5. Circular motion in a magnetic field Test Q13(b)

A particle of mass m and positive charge q moves on a circular path with speed v in a vacuum. A uniform magnetic field B is directed into the plane of the page.

The resultant (centripetal) force

20At the position shown in Fig. 5.1, which way does the magnetic force on the particle act?
Left-hand rule: first finger into the page (field), second finger to the right (motion of a positive charge).
Up the page, towards the centre of the circle.
21What is the resultant force on the particle? Give an expression for its size, and explain why it is the centripetal force.
Find the direction of the force using the left-hand rule at the top, left side, bottom, and right side of the pathway.
The only force acting is the magnetic force, so it is the resultant force: F = qvB. It is always perpendicular to the velocity and points towards the centre of the circle, so it provides the centripetal force: qvB = mv2/r.
22How could the size of this force be changed? For each change, say what happens to the radius of the circle.
From F = qvB, the force increases if q, v or B increases.
• Larger B or larger q: the force is bigger at the same speed, so the radius gets smaller.
• Larger v: the force increases, but the centripetal force needed (mv2/r) increases even faster, so the radius gets larger (r ∝ v).
• Larger m does not change the force, but the radius increases.
23The magnetic field strength is doubled and the speed stays the same. What happens to the radius of the path?
Equate the magnetic force to the centripetal force and rearrange for r.
It halves, since r = mv/(qB) ∝ 1/B.

The exam questions

24Show that the radius R of the circular path is given by R = mv/(qB). [1] Test Q13(b)(i)
The magnetic force provides the centripetal force: qvB = mv2/R ✓. Rearranging gives R = mv/(qB).
25Suggest why the speed of the particle stays constant, even though a force acts on the particle. [1] Test Q13(b)(ii)
The force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work on the particle. It changes only the direction of the velocity, not the speed ✓.
Mark scheme: do not accept a reference to centripetal acceleration only.

6. Adding air Test Q13(c)

Stage 1 — no magnetic field

26A charged particle travels in a straight line at constant speed through a vacuum. Air is now let in. What happens to the particle, and why?
In the vacuum there is no resultant force, so the particle keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed (Newton’s first law). With air present, it collides with air molecules. Each collision transfers some of its kinetic energy to the molecules, which acts as a resistive force opposite to its motion. So the particle slows down.

Stage 2 — back to the magnetic field

27Now think about the particle from Section 5, moving in a circle in the magnetic field. What happens to its path when air is let in?
The particle slows down. Use R = mv/(qB): what happens to R as v falls?
C. As the particle slows down, R = mv/(qB) gets smaller all the time. The particle never comes back round to the same point, so it spirals inwards.

Stage 3 — the exam question

28The particle in (b), now moving in a region of magnetic field in air, follows the path shown. Explain the shape of this path. [2] Test Q13(c)
The particle loses (kinetic) energy in collisions with air molecules, so its speed decreases ✓. Since R = mv/(qB) and m, q and B stay the same, the radius decreases, giving a spiral that tightens inwards ✓.
Mark scheme: the second mark is scored only if the first is scored.
Examiner’s note: answers that tried to explain the shape only through a change in the size of the centripetal force were confusing. Start from the loss of speed.

Glossary

Gravitational potential, Vg
Work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to a point. Unit: J kg−1 = m2 s−2.
Dot / cross notation
⊙ = out of the page (towards you); ⊗ = into the page (away from you).
Right-hand grip rule
Thumb along the conventional current; curled fingers give the direction of the magnetic field lines around the wire.
Fleming’s left-hand rule
First finger = field, second finger = current (direction of motion of a positive charge), thumb = force.
Centripetal force
The resultant force, directed towards the centre of a circle, needed for circular motion: F = mv2/r.
