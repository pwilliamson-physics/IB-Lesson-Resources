Magnetic Field Around a Wire

1. The field around a current-carrying wire

Any wire carrying a current is surrounded by a magnetic field. The field lines are circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it. Because these circles stick in and out of the page when the wire is drawn on the page, we show the field using two symbols:

Dot and cross notation: a dot • means the field points out of the page (towards you — like seeing the tip of an arrow coming at you). A cross × means the field points into the page (away from you — like seeing the tail feathers of an arrow going away).
Right-hand grip rule. Grip the wire with your right hand, thumb pointing in the direction of the conventional current. Your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field lines.

Check your understanding

1Use the right-hand grip rule to explain why Fig. 1.1 shows dots on the left of the wire and crosses on the right.
Point the right thumb up the page (the direction of conventional current). The fingers curl around the wire: on the left side they come up out of the page towards you (•), and on the right side they go back into the page (×).
2In Fig. 1.1 the symbols get further apart the further they are from the wire. What is this telling you about the magnetic field?
The closer together the field lines (symbols), the stronger the field. Wider spacing further away shows that the magnetic field strength B decreases with distance from the wire. The rest of this workbook works out exactly how it decreases.

2. What does the field strength depend on?

Magnetic fields are produced by moving charges. A wire connected in a circuit has lots of quantities we could measure — the potential difference across it, its resistance, the power it dissipates, the current through it. Only one of these is directly responsible for the field.

Check your understanding

3The magnetic field strength B around the wire originally depends on which quantity?
B ∝ I

Double the current → double the field strength.

A magnetic field is produced by moving charge. Which of these four quantities measures the rate at which charge flows?
Current, I. Current is the rate of flow of charge, and moving charge is what creates the magnetic field. More charge passing each second → a stronger field, so B ∝ I.
4A wire is connected to a 12 V supply, but there is a break elsewhere in the circuit. Is there a magnetic field around the wire? Explain why V, R and P only affect B indirectly.
No — with a break in the circuit no current flows, so no charges are moving and there is no magnetic field, even though there is a p.d. across the supply. V, R and P can only change B by changing the current (e.g. I = V/R), so current is the quantity B really depends on.

3. How does the field change with distance? A shells model

Now look straight down on the wire, so we see its cross-section with the current coming out of the page towards us (as in Fig. 1.2). In this model, the current produces a fixed "amount" of magnetic field, shown by a set of coloured dots. The same amount of field has to be shared out around every circle surrounding the wire — so each ring-shaped shell gets the same number of dots.

The magnetic field strength at a point is represented by how concentrated the dots are there: dots per unit area.

5Shell 1 is a circle of radius r, with area πr². Shell 2 is the ring between r and 2r. How many times bigger is the area of shell 2 than shell 1?
times
Area of the ring = (area of the big circle, radius 2r) − (area of the small circle, radius r).
Area of shell 2 = π(2r)² − πr² = 4πr² − πr² = 3πr². That is 3 times the area of shell 1.
6Shell 5 is the ring between 4r and 5r. How many times bigger is its area than shell 1?
times
Same method as question 5, using radii 5r and 4r. The "Shell area / r²" column in the table can help you check.
Area of shell 5 = π(5r)² − π(4r)² = 25πr² − 16πr² = 9πr², so 9 times the area of shell 1.
7Every shell holds the same number of dots (Fig. 3.2 is flat). Describe and explain what happens to the dot concentration — the field strength — as you move away from the wire (Fig. 3.3).
The same amount of field is shared out over a bigger and bigger area (1, 3, 5, 7, 9 times shell 1's area), so the dots become more spread out and the field strength falls with distance. Each shell's area is its circumference at the middle radius (2πr) × its thickness (r), so the dot density is the same number of dots ÷ 2πr — the field strength is inversely proportional to the distance r.
Reading the curve: the dashed curve in Fig. 3.3 passes through the shell values. Use it to compare the field strength at 1r, 2r, 3r, 4r and 5r for the next two questions. You can type a fraction (like 1/3) or a decimal.
8The field strength at a distance of 1r from the wire is B1. The field strength at 2r is ______ × B1.
At 2r the same amount of field is spread around a circle with twice the circumference.
The circumference doubles (2π × 2r = 2 × 2πr), so the field strength halves: 0.5 × B1.
9The field strength at 4r is ______ × B1.
How many times bigger is the circumference at 4r than at 1r?
The circumference is 4 times bigger, so the field strength is a quarter: 0.25 × B1.
10Which relationship describes how the field strength B depends on the distance r from the wire?
B ∝ 12πr

The field is shared out around a circle of circumference 2πr.

Look at your answers to questions 8 and 9: doubling the distance halved B; four times the distance gave a quarter.
B ∝ 1/r — doubling r halves B. More precisely, the same field is spread around a circumference of 2πr, so B ∝ 1/(2πr).

4. Putting the two together

We now have two results for the field around a long straight wire:

From Section 2: B ∝ I   (more current → stronger field)
From Section 3: B ∝ 1/(2πr)   (field shared around a bigger circle → weaker field)

Since B is proportional to each of these, it is proportional to their product:

B ∝ I2πr

Check your understanding

11Explain, in your own words, where the "2πr" in this relationship comes from.
The field lines form circles around the wire. At a distance r, the field produced by the current is shared out around a circle of circumference 2πr — so the bigger that circle, the weaker the field at any one point on it.
12The current in a wire is doubled, and you measure the field at twice the distance. The field strength changes by a factor of ______.
Deal with each change separately: what does doubling I do to B? What does doubling r do? Then multiply the two factors.
Doubling I → × 2. Doubling r → × ½. Overall: 2 × ½ = 1 — the field strength is unchanged.
13The current is tripled and the distance from the wire is halved. The field strength changes by a factor of ______.
Halving r halves the denominator 2πr. What does that do to the whole fraction?
Tripling I → × 3. Halving r → × 2. Overall: 3 × 2 = 6 times stronger.

5. The constant of proportionality: μ0

To turn a proportionality into an equation, we need a constant. For the magnetic field in a vacuum (and, to a very good approximation, in air), this constant is the permeability of free space, μ0 ("mu-nought").

Permeability of free space, μ0: a constant that describes how readily a magnetic field is set up in a vacuum by a current. μ0 = 4π × 10−7 T m A−1 (≈ 1.26 × 10−6 T m A−1). It is given in your data booklet.

Replacing "∝" with "= μ0 ×" completes the equation:

B = μ0I2πr
The finished result. B is the magnetic field strength (T), I the current (A), r the perpendicular distance from the wire (m) and μ0 the permeability of free space. This is the equation in your data booklet for the field around a long, straight current-carrying wire.
Handy shortcut: μ0/(2π) = 2 × 10−7 T m A−1 exactly.
Worked example 5.1

A long straight wire carries a current of 2.0 A. Find the magnetic field strength 1.0 cm from the wire.

Answer:
Convert the distance: r = 1.0 cm = 0.010 m.
B = μ0I / (2πr)
B = (4π × 10−7 × 2.0) / (2π × 0.010)

Typing standard form: write your answer like 5.0 x 10^-5. You can also use × or *, and brackets like 10^(-5) or 10(-5); spaces don't matter. Calculator E-notation (5.0E-5) and 10-5 without a ^ or brackets won't be accepted.

Check your understanding

14A long straight wire carries a current of 5.0 A. Calculate the magnetic field strength 2.0 cm from the wire. Give your answer in standard form.
T
Convert 2.0 cm to metres first. Then substitute into B = μ0I/(2πr), as in the worked example.
r = 0.020 m.
B = (4π × 10−7 × 5.0) / (2π × 0.020) = (2 × 10−7 × 5.0) / 0.020 = 5.0 × 10−5 T
15The magnetic field strength 5.0 cm from a long straight wire is 4.0 × 10−6 T. Calculate the current in the wire.
A
Rearrange first: I = 2πrB / μ0. Remember r in metres.
I = 2πrB / μ0 = (2π × 0.050 × 4.0 × 10−6) / (4π × 10−7) = 1.0 A
16A wire carries a current of 10 A. At what distance from the wire is the magnetic field strength 2.0 × 10−5 T? Give your answer in metres.
m
Rearrange for r: r = μ0I / (2πB).
r = μ0I / (2πB) = (4π × 10−7 × 10) / (2π × 2.0 × 10−5) = 0.10 m (10 cm)
17Rearrange B = μ0I/(2πr) to make μ0 the subject, and use it to show that the unit of μ0 is T m A−1.
μ0 = 2πrB / I. 2π has no unit, so the unit is (m × T) / A = T m A−1.

Glossary

Magnetic field strength, B
A measure of how strong a magnetic field is at a point; unit tesla (T). Shown by how close together field lines are.
Conventional current
The direction positive charge would flow: from the positive terminal of the supply, round the circuit, to the negative terminal.
Dot (•) / cross (×) notation
Symbols for a field pointing out of the page (•) or into the page (×).
Right-hand grip rule
Thumb along the conventional current; curled fingers give the direction of the magnetic field around the wire.
Permeability of free space, μ0
The constant of proportionality in B = μ0I/(2πr); μ0 = 4π × 10−7 T m A−1.
